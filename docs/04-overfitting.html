When it goes wrong: overfitting, caught in the act

Chapter 3's loss curve looked like a triumph. It wasn't. A model can drive its training loss down two ways: by learning the task, or by memorizing the answers. Only held-out data can tell the two apart — and this run's held-out numbers deliver a clear verdict.

An intuition for the numbers: if you raise e to the power of the cross-entropy loss, you get perplexity — a rough count of how many next-token choices the model is torn between. e0.71 ≈ 2 — on memorized data the model hesitates between about two plausible next tokens. e1.43 ≈ 4.2 — on new data it hesitates between about four. The model became about twice as sure on questions it will never be asked again.

Why it happened — four compounding causes

None of these is a bug. Each is a defensible-looking config choice; together they guarantee memorization. (This diagnosis follows the project's own post-mortem, outputs/biomistral-medical/training_analysis.md.)

  1. Tiny dataset — 720 samples. A 7B-parameter model can absorb the entire training set's surface patterns in a single pass. There is simply not enough signal to keep learning from for three passes.
  2. Three epochs. Chapter 3 showed the tell: the sharp loss drop at the epoch-3 boundary. Improvement on the third re-read of identical data is recognition, not learning.
  3. High adapter capacity — rank 32 ≈ 84M parameters. That's about 116,000 trainable parameters per training sample. The adapter had far more room than the task needed, and spare capacity gets spent on memorization.
  4. No safety net. With evaluation only every 100 steps and no early stopping, nothing could notice the eval loss turning upward — the run just kept going to its scheduled end.

The fix list — what to change and why

Perspective: an eval loss of 1.43 is not catastrophic for a 7B model on specialized text — the model still works. The alarm is the direction: eval loss rising while train loss falls means every further step makes the deployed model slightly worse. The habit to build is to watch the gap and its trend, never the train curve alone.

The general lesson

This failure mode is the single most common one in small-scale finetuning, because the defaults that demo well — small curated dataset, several epochs, generous rank — are exactly the recipe for it. The instinct to build is: train loss is what the optimizer sees; eval loss is what your users see. When the two part ways, believe the one your users see.
